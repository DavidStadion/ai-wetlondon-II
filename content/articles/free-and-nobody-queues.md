There is a particular kind of London failure where you do the sensible thing, decide to go somewhere free and indoors, and end up standing outside the Natural History Museum in the rain behind four hundred other people who had the identical thought at the identical moment.

The famous free museums are famous for good reason. They are also, on a wet weekend, indistinguishable from an airport.

So here are nine that cost nothing, keep you dry, and where the most crowded thing you will encounter is a volunteer who wants to tell you about the roof.

## 1. London Mithraeum

A Roman temple to a god nobody worships any more, sitting in the basement of Bloomberg's European headquarters, reached by descending through a very expensive lobby while a man in a suit holds a door for you.

They flood it with mist and voices in Latin. It takes twenty minutes and it is one of the strangest things under the City. Free, but book a slot, because the one thing they do control is numbers.

[London Mithraeum](/venue/london-mithraeum)

## 2. Sir John Soane's Museum

An architect's house, left exactly as he arranged it in 1837, on the condition that nothing be moved. So nothing has been.

The result is a building stuffed to the ceiling with marble, mirrors and a genuine Egyptian sarcophagus, arranged by a man who clearly could not stop. There is a picture room where the walls open outward to reveal more paintings behind the paintings. It should not be free. It is.

[Sir John Soane's Museum](/venue/sir-john-soanes-museum)

## 3. Two Temple Place

An Astor mansion off the Embankment that opens to the public for one exhibition a winter and then shuts again like a clam.

Carved staircases, stained glass, hammerbeam ceiling, the lot. Because it is only open a few months a year, most Londoners have never heard of it, which is precisely why you can stand in the middle of it on a Saturday afternoon with nobody in shot.

[Two Temple Place](/venue/two-temple-place)

## 4. V&A East Storehouse

Not a museum. A working store, with half a million objects on open shelves and no glass between you and any of them.

You can order almost anything out of storage to look at up close, which is the single best free thing that has happened to London in years. It opened this year and Stratford has not quite caught up with what it has.

[V&A East Storehouse](/venue/v-a-east-storehouse)

## 5. Guildhall Library

A public reference library the City has been running since the 1420s, which is a sentence worth reading twice.

Enormous London history collection, high ceilings, and the specific silence of a room where people are concentrating. Thousands of people walk past the entrance every day on their way to buy a sandwich.

[Guildhall Library](/venue/guildhall-library)

## 6. Peckham Library

The copper-clad one that won the Stirling Prize, and the only library that has ever managed it.

The reading room is cantilevered out above the street, so you sit in a bright box in the air with the rain going sideways past the glass, and nobody minds how long you stay. Bring a book you have been failing to finish.

[Peckham Library](/venue/peckham-library)

## 7. Wiener Holocaust Library

The oldest Holocaust archive in the world, with free exhibitions on the ground floor and a reading room upstairs.

It is quiet, serious, and open to anyone who walks in off Russell Square. Not a light afternoon, and not pretending to be. Some places earn an hour of your attention rather than competing for it.

[Wiener Holocaust Library](/venue/wiener-holocaust-library)

## 8. Barbican Conservatory

A jungle hidden inside the Barbican's concrete. Palms, ferns, koi, and a glass roof with London weather hammering on it a few feet above your head.

It opens on selected days only, which is the single most Barbican fact about the Barbican. Check before you go. It is worth arranging your weekend around.

[Barbican Conservatory](/venue/barbican-conservatory)

## 9. National Poetry Library

Fifth floor of the Southbank Centre. The largest collection of modern poetry anywhere in the world, free, open to everyone, and almost always with a spare chair.

You do not have to like poetry. You have to like sitting somewhere warm and dry with something to read while the Thames does its grey thing outside.

[National Poetry Library](/venue/national-poetry-library)

## The thing they have in common

None of these are secret. They are all listed, signposted and open to the public. They are simply not the first thing that comes up when a wet Saturday arrives and everyone reaches for the same three names.

That is usually all "hidden London" means: somewhere perfectly visible that nobody happened to mention.
