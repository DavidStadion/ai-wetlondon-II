There are two kinds of London rain and they need different answers.

Summer rain is an inconvenience. You get wet, you go inside, you dry off in twenty minutes and the afternoon continues. Nobody's plans are really ruined.

Then around the middle of October it changes into the other thing, where the rain is four degrees and horizontal and gets inside your collar, and the only meaningful question about any destination is how warm it is when you arrive. Interest becomes secondary. A merely adequate museum that is properly heated beats a brilliant one with a draughty hall, and anyone who says otherwise has not stood in the queue.

So this list is sorted on heat. Some of these are excellent and some are simply warm, and in December that distinction stops mattering.

## The warmest room in London: Barbican Conservatory

Free, a tropical glasshouse hidden inside the Barbican's concrete, palms and ferns and koi.

It is humid. That is the whole recommendation. Walking into thirty percent humidity and green leaves in the second week of January does something to a person that no gallery can match. Open on selected days only, so check before you go.

[Barbican Conservatory](/venue/barbican-conservatory)

## Actual hot water: Ironmonger Row Baths

Old Street, and one of the last proper municipal bathhouses in the city.

Turkish baths, steam and hot rooms, at public-pool prices rather than spa prices. The building has been doing this since the 1930s and has the tiling to prove it. If the cold has properly got into you, nothing else on this list competes.

[Ironmonger Row Baths](/venue/ironmonger-row-baths)

## Five floors of heated browsing: Waterstones Piccadilly

Free, warm, calm, and dangerously easy to lose time in.

Bookshops are the great unacknowledged winter infrastructure of this city. Nobody minds how long you stay, there are chairs, and there is a bar at the top for when browsing turns into an evening.

[Waterstones Piccadilly](/venue/waterstones-piccadilly)

## The most beautiful way to stay dry: Liberty London

Free to walk into, and the building is the reason to go.

Mock-Tudor, built from the timbers of two ships, and arranged around a warm wooden atrium that in December is full of decorations and people who are also not buying anything. You will not be able to afford the scarves. Look at them anyway.

[Liberty London](/venue/liberty-london)

## Warm, free and enormous: National Gallery

Free, Trafalgar Square, and directly on top of Charing Cross.

Included on the grounds of pure heated square footage. You can spend three hours crossing it slowly, and the specific winter advantage is that the entrance is about ninety seconds from the station, so you arrive warm rather than recovering.

[National Gallery](/venue/national-gallery)

## The one with the fire and the nonsense: Mr Fogg's Society of Exploration

Free to enter, a Victorian explorer's parlour done as a cocktail bar.

Absolutely ridiculous, deliberately so, and cluttered with taxidermy and expedition clutter in a way that makes it feel like the warmest room in the building. Best in the cold, when the whole conceit of having just returned from somewhere hostile makes sense.

[Mr Fogg's Society of Exploration](/venue/mr-foggs-society-of-exploration)

## Small, warm and rarely full: Dulwich Picture Gallery

South London, England's oldest public art gallery, and a permanent collection worth the train.

A proper winter destination: quiet rooms, a good cafe, and few enough people that you never queue for a coat. Going somewhere slightly out of the way in bad weather is its own small pleasure, because everybody else has stayed in zone one.

[Dulwich Picture Gallery](/venue/dulwich-picture-gallery)

## A very old cinema with the heating on: Phoenix Cinema

North London, showing films since 1912, cosy and properly local.

There is a version of a winter afternoon that is just a warm dark room and a film at three o'clock, and this is the best building in London to have it in.

[Phoenix Cinema](/venue/phoenix-cinema)

## Underground, which is the point: London Mithraeum

Free, in the basement of Bloomberg's European headquarters in the City.

A Roman temple, seven metres below the street, reached by descending through a very expensive lobby. Being underground in winter is underrated: no wind, no draught, and a constant temperature that the surface has not managed since September. Book a slot, because the one thing they control is numbers.

[London Mithraeum](/venue/london-mithraeum)
